1. Introduction and learning objectives

An light-dependent resistor (LDR) changes resistance when the light falling on it changes. In common LDRs, resistance is high in darkness and decreases as illumination increases. A voltage divider converts this resistance change into a voltage change. An operational amplifier (op-amp) then scales the sensor voltage to a more useful level.

Sensor stage

Converts light level into a varying electrical voltage.

Amplifier stage

Uses negative feedback to provide a predictable voltage gain.

Control interface

The amplified signal can feed a comparator, ADC, or control circuit. A driver stage is needed for a lamp, relay, or motor.

Prerequisites and terminology

TermMeaning
LDRA light-sensitive resistor; its resistance generally falls as illumination rises.
Voltage dividerTwo series impedances that produce a fraction of the supply voltage at their junction.
Op-ampA differential amplifier often used with feedback to set circuit gain.
Negative feedbackFeeds part of the output back to the inverting input to stabilize gain.
GainOutput voltage divided by input voltage, Av=Vo/V_i, for this voltage amplifier.
SaturationOutput reaches a limit set by the op-amp and its supply rails.
Design idea: the LDR itself is not a voltage source. Pair it with a fixed resistor to create a voltage that changes with light.

3. Working principle

  1. Light changes the LDR resistance. More light usually means lower LDR resistance; darkness means higher resistance.
  2. The divider converts resistance into voltage. In your Multisim circuit, a 10 kΩ potentiometer creates the adjustable test voltage. For real light sensing, substitute an LDR voltage divider; with the LDR above the junction and a fixed resistor below, the junction voltage generally rises as light increases.
  3. The op-amp receives the sensor voltage. The divider node goes to the non-inverting (+) input.
  4. Feedback sets the gain. Rf connects the output to the inverting (−) input, while Rg connects that input to ground.
  5. The output is a scaled signal. Ideally, Vout = 11 × Vin for your shown 100 kΩ / 10 kΩ feedback network, until the output approaches its practical limit.
  6. A later stage makes the decision. A comparator or microcontroller can decide whether a light should switch on. This amplifier alone does not provide an adjustable switching threshold.

DC and AC behaviour

DC: steady illumination produces a steady divider voltage and, while the op-amp remains in its linear range, a steady amplified output.

Changing light: a changing illumination level causes a changing sensor voltage. The output follows that change within the circuit's bandwidth and slew-rate limits. The LDR itself can respond relatively slowly compared with a photodiode.

Advantages and limitations

Advantages

  • Simple and low cost
  • Adjustable signal scaling through resistor values
  • Useful for light-level sensing and later control stages

Limitations

  • LDR resistance varies between devices and with temperature
  • Amplifier output clips near supply limits
  • Noise and ambient-light changes can affect the signal
  • A separate threshold/driver stage may be required

5. Key equations and derivation

A. Voltage divider

For this connection, the LDR is above the junction and the fixed resistor is below it.

Vs=VCC·RfRLDR+Rf

Here, Vs is the sensor voltage in volts, VCC is the divider supply in volts, Rf is the fixed divider resistor in ohms, and RLDR is the LDR resistance in ohms. As RLDR falls, Vs rises.

B. Non-inverting op-amp gain

Av=VoVs=1+RFRG

For RF=10\ k\Ω and RG=10\ k\Ω, the ideal gain is 1+10/10=2. Thus Vo=2Vs, provided the op-amp operates linearly and the input/output limits are respected.

C. Combined ideal transfer

Vo=1+RFRG·VCC·RfRLDR+Rf

Capital RF,RG denote the op-amp feedback network; lowercase Rf here denotes the fixed sensor-divider resistor. These are separate components despite similar notation.

D. Gain in decibels

Av,dB=20·log10|Av|

For a voltage gain of 2, 20 × log₁₀(2) ≈ 6.02 dB.

These equations assume the op-amp is not saturated, the feedback is negative, and input currents are small. Real devices have input-offset voltage, finite bandwidth, limited output swing, and other non-ideal behaviour.

6. How to build and test it in NI Multisim

  1. Create a new design and place an LDR (or a variable resistor/potentiometer for a controlled test), a 10 kΩ fixed resistor, an LM358/suitable op-amp, two 10 kΩ feedback resistors, DC sources, and ground.
  2. Build the divider: +5 V → LDR → sensor junction → 10 kΩ → ground. Label the junction Vs.
  3. Connect Vs to the op-amp non-inverting (+) input.
  4. Connect a 10 kΩ resistor from op-amp output to the inverting (−) input, and another 10 kΩ resistor from the inverting input to ground.
  5. Power the op-amp according to its model. For the example, use +12 V and ground for a suitable single-supply model such as LM358. Check the symbol's power pins and model requirements.
  6. Attach voltage probes to Vs and Vout. Use a DC operating-point analysis or multimeter first. Then vary the LDR parameter or potentiometer setting and record the voltages.
  7. Expected trend: as LDR resistance falls, Vs rises; Vout should rise at approximately twice Vs until it reaches its output limit.

Using a potentiometer as a test substitute: a potentiometer lets you vary resistance manually and verify that the amplifier responds. It does not automatically respond to real light; replace it with an LDR for the actual light-dependent function.

7. Solved numerical examples

Example 1 — sensor voltage

Given: VCC=5V, RLDR=10k\Ω, Rf=10k\Ω.

Formula: Vs=VCC\Rf/(R_LDR + Rf).

Substitute: Vs = 5 × 10/(10 + 10) = 2.5 V.

Answer: sensor output is 2.5 V.

Example 2 — amplifier output

Given: Vs=2.5V, RF=10k\Ω, RG=10k\Ω.

Av=1+10/10=2, so Vo=2\×2.5=5V.

Answer: ideal output is 5 V, which is within the illustrative LM358 circuit's practical range.

Example 3 — GATE-style combined calculation

Given: VCC=5V, Rf=10k\Ω, RLDR=5k\Ω, and amplifier gain Av=2.

Vs = 5 × 10/(5 + 10) = 3.33 V. Then Vo = 2 × 3.33 = 6.67 V.

Answer: ideal output is approximately 6.67 V. A real result may differ due to device limits and model behaviour.

9. Core electronics interview preparation

1. What is an LDR?

A light-sensitive resistor whose resistance generally decreases as incident light increases. It is commonly used in ambient-light sensing.

2. Why use a voltage divider with an LDR?

The LDR changes resistance, not directly a standard voltage output. The divider converts the resistance change into a measurable voltage.

3. Why choose a non-inverting amplifier?

It provides a positive voltage gain, has high input impedance, and does not invert the sensor signal.

4. How is the gain calculated?

For the standard non-inverting configuration, Av=1+RF/RG, assuming ideal linear operation.

5. What does negative feedback do?

It feeds a fraction of the output back to the inverting input, stabilizing closed-loop gain and improving linearity within the op-amp's operating limits.

6. Why can the output become a flat line?

The input may be constant, the output may be saturated, the feedback may be miswired, power may be missing, or the simulation may not include a changing input.

7. Why can't the op-amp drive a relay directly?

A relay coil may require more current than the op-amp can safely supply. Use a transistor or MOSFET driver and a flyback diode for a DC relay coil.

8. What is the difference between an amplifier and a comparator?

An amplifier is intended to scale a signal in its linear region. A comparator compares two voltages and switches its output state based on which is higher.

9. How can the circuit be calibrated?

Measure sensor voltage at known light levels, adjust the divider or gain if needed, and set the downstream comparator threshold or software threshold for the desired switching point.

10. What practical issues affect accuracy?

LDR tolerance and response time, ambient-light geometry, resistor tolerance, op-amp offset/bias current, noise, supply variation, and output swing.

11. Why use a potentiometer during testing?

It gives a controllable resistance change for checking the circuit response. It is a convenient test substitute, but it cannot sense light by itself.

12. How would you reduce flicker near a light-switching threshold?

Use a comparator with hysteresis (Schmitt-trigger behaviour), filtering, or a suitable time delay in the control logic.

Applications and industry relevance

  • Automatic night lamps and street-light controllers
  • Display brightness and ambient-light sensing
  • Light-activated alarms and simple optical detection
  • Analog sensor front ends feeding an ADC or comparator

Engineering note: for precise or fast optical measurements, a photodiode or phototransistor with a suitable amplifier may be preferred. For a simple low-cost light-dependent controller, an LDR can be adequate.
